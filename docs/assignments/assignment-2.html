DV Assignment 2 (Group Project)

Instructions

For the second and final assignment of the Data Visualization module, which you complete in a group, the challenge is to transform raw data into a clear and compelling story. Your goal is to design a visualization using the DUO doorstroomtoets (transfer test) data that highlights meaningful patterns and provides actionable insights. You can choose one of four research questions (substantiated below), or formulate your own (RQ5). Each of RQ1–RQ4 comes with a code chunk that prepares the relevant data. You are expected to do some further data preparation and/or filtering of this data to obtain clean, reliable results. For RQ1–RQ4, you are not expected to find data from other sources or perform complicated data manipulations to derive a variable – focus on using the data given to create a sound data visualization.

The assignment consists of two parts:

Part 2: Final report

The report

You will write the assignment in R Markdown (or Quarto), to produce a report-style document of no more than 1000 words. The final version should be compiled into .html format.

The report should be a description of the process that led to your final data visualization. You should include:

  • An explanation of your research question and the target audience for your visualization. What did you want to communicate?

  • A description of your exploratory process, including at least one exploratory graph. This can be the graph that you created in Part 1, or an updated one. While this graph is intended to illustrate your thought process, its aesthetic- and communicative quality will not be graded. However, we will grade the quality of the report as a whole, which includes communication about how the exploratory graph helped you to understand the data, and influenced your choices for the final, explanatory, data visualization.

  • Your final, explanatory, data visualization. Clearly mark which one of your visualizations this is. Include the visualization, along with an explanation of what it aims to communicate. Importantly, what can you conclude from this visualization? Why is this the best way to communicate this conclusion? How robust are your findings? This should follow logically from the introduction of your report (research question & exploration).

Grading

The final report will be graded on the following elements:

  • Aesthetics [30%]: The overall visual quality of the plot. Is it clear and legible, with no unnecessary clutter? Do choices like color, labels, or scales enhance readability and interpretation?
  • Communication [30%]: How well the visualization conveys its intended message. Can the main point be understood quickly with minimal explanation? Do the title, labels, and layout support effective communication?
  • Description [20%]: The quality of the written report. Does it clearly state the research question, what you wanted to communicate, and why this visualization is appropriate? Does it provide a concise interpretation of what can be concluded from the data? Is it explanatory?
  • Creativity and Complexity [10%]: The thoughtfulness and depth of the visualization. Does it go beyond a default chart type when appropriate? Were creative or more complex techniques used in a way that adds value without obscuring the message?
  • Code Quality and Styling [10%]: The clarity and reproducibility of the R code. Does the code run without errors and generate the submitted plot? Is it well-structured, readable, and consistent with good style practices (i.e., lintr-proof)?

Levels of visualization

Your chosen research question falls into one of three levels: analytics, inference, or prediction. Each level has different expectations in terms of how the visualization should be constructed and what role statistical modeling plays.

Analytics

Analytics is about describing and comparing what is happening in the data, often in a way that is directly actionable for a practitioner. At this level, we expect you to create clear, actionable visualizations that are easy to interpret for a practitioner (e.g. a school board or municipal education officer). This should rely less on statistical modeling and more on effective communication of patterns in the data. You do, however, need to make sure that the patterns communicated are reliable.

Inference

Inference goes one step further: it aims to explain differences or relationships in the data, often by comparing groups or testing hypotheses. We expect a visualization at this level to be supplemented with statistical tests or models (e.g. significance tests). The visualization should not only show patterns but also provide evidence for why the observed differences matter.

Prediction

Prediction focuses on using current information to forecast outcomes. At this level, you should fit and interpret a predictive model (e.g., a linear or logistic regression). The visualization should clearly communicate how well the model performs, what predictors matter, and what can be learned from the model’s outcomes.

Data and codebook

All research questions use the DUO open data that you already worked with in the worksheets: eindscores, referentieniveaus, schooladviezen and schoolweging. Start from the starter project: its scripts/01-get-data.R downloads and loads all four files.

  • The data: background & codebook explains where the data comes from, the fairness debate these questions come from, every column in the four files, and the Dutch terms you’ll run into (reference levels, school advice tracks, schoolweging).
Hidden counts: "<5"

DUO hides any count below 5 and writes the text "<5" instead. In referentieniveaus and schooladviezen this happens a lot, because students are split over many small categories. If you simply drop the schools with a "<5" somewhere, almost no schools are left over.

The code below therefore replaces "<5" with a number of your choice (2.5, the midpoint of 1–4, by default). This is an assumption, so check whether your conclusion changes when you use 1 or 4 instead, and report this in your robustness section.

Run the chunk below first, whichever research question you choose. It loads the data and creates the helper objects that the RQ-specific chunks use.

Data preparation (all RQs): 
# Load packages and data. Run this from your own project (the starter
# template): 01-get-data.R downloads the data if needed, and loads
# eindscores, referentieniveaus, schooladviezen and schoolweging.
source(here::here("scripts", "00-packages.R"))
source(here::here("scripts", "01-get-data.R"))

# DUO writes "<5" for counts under 5. This helper turns a count column into
# numbers, replacing "<5" with `value`. Rerun your analysis with 1 and 4 to
# check that your conclusion doesn't depend on this choice.
suppressed_value <- 2.5

parse_count <- function(x, value = suppressed_value) {
  x <- as.character(x)
  as.numeric(replace(x, x == "<5", value))
}

# Which test provider did each school use? Only eindscores has this. Most
# schools use one provider; for the few that use more, we keep the provider
# that tested the most students.
school_provider <- eindscores |>
  pivot_longer(
    cols = ends_with("_AANTAL"),
    names_to = "provider",
    values_to = "n_tested"
  ) |>
  mutate(
    provider = str_remove(provider, "_AANTAL"),
    n_tested = parse_count(n_tested)
  ) |>
  filter(n_tested > 0) |>
  slice_max(
    n_tested,
    by = c(INSTELLINGSCODE, VESTIGINGSCODE),
    with_ties = FALSE
  ) |>
  select(INSTELLINGSCODE, VESTIGINGSCODE, provider, n_tested)

# Schoolweging per school. OVT combines INSTELLINGSCODE and a location code
# (e.g. "00AP|C1"), so we split it and summarise per INSTELLINGSCODE, which
# is what we can join on (see the comments at the bottom of 01-get-data.R).
school_weging <- schoolweging |>
  separate_wider_delim(
    OVT,
    delim = "|",
    names = c("INSTELLINGSCODE", "location")
  ) |>
  summarise(
    schoolweging = weighted.mean(schoolweging, aantal_leerlingen),
    spreiding = weighted.mean(spreiding, aantal_leerlingen),
    n_students_school = sum(aantal_leerlingen),
    .by = INSTELLINGSCODE
  )

# Reference levels: share of students per school reaching each level.
# Maths (REKENEN): basic level 1F, target level 1S.
# Reading (LV, leesvaardigheid): basic level 1F, target level 2F.
ref_levels <- referentieniveaus |>
  mutate(across(REKENEN_LAGER1F:TV_2F, parse_count)) |>
  mutate(
    n_maths = REKENEN_LAGER1F + REKENEN_1F + REKENEN_1S + REKENEN_2F,
    pct_maths_1f = 1 - REKENEN_LAGER1F / n_maths,
    pct_maths_1s = (REKENEN_1S + REKENEN_2F) / n_maths,
    n_reading = LV_LAGER1F + LV_1F + LV_2F,
    pct_reading_1f = 1 - LV_LAGER1F / n_reading,
    pct_reading_2f = LV_2F / n_reading
  )

# School advice: number of students per secondary-school track, plus the share
# of students with a HAVO-or-higher advice (you may define this differently!)
advice <- schooladviezen |>
  mutate(across(VSO:ADVIES_NIET_MOGELIJK, parse_count)) |>
  mutate(
    n_advice = rowSums(pick(VSO:ADVIES_NIET_MOGELIJK)),
    pct_havo_plus = (HAVO + HAVO_VWO + VWO) / n_advice
  )

Research Questions

RQ1. Which schools in a municipality are on track for reading and maths?

Expand Instructions

Level: Analytics

Target audience: a municipal education officer or a school board.

Instruction: Choose one municipality, and visualize how each of its primary schools performs on the reference levels for maths (1F and 1S) and reading (1F and 2F). The visualization should be useful for the municipality: they should be able to see at a glance which schools are on track and which are falling behind. You can choose one of two ways to define “on track”:

  1. National benchmark: compare each school to the national average share of students reaching each level.

  2. Comparable-schools benchmark: compare each school to schools with a similar student population, i.e. a similar schoolweging. A school with many disadvantaged students and 40% at 1S may be doing better than expected, while a school with very few disadvantaged students and the same 40% may not be.

Good municipalities to start with are Haarlem (38 schools), Leiden (25) or Delft (25). Larger cities like Amsterdam (200+ schools) are possible, but you will need to think harder about how to keep the plot readable.

Data: 
# Run the data preparation chunk above first.

# Choose your municipality (see unique(ref_levels$GEMEENTENAAM))
my_municipality <- "Haarlem"

# National benchmark: average share per level across all schools
national <- ref_levels |>
  summarise(across(starts_with("pct_"), \(x) mean(x, na.rm = TRUE)))

# Reference levels of the schools in your municipality, with schoolweging
rq1_data <- ref_levels |>
  filter(GEMEENTENAAM == my_municipality) |>
  left_join(school_weging, by = "INSTELLINGSCODE") |>
  select(
    INSTELLINGSCODE, VESTIGINGSCODE, INSTELLINGSNAAM_VESTIGING,
    n_maths, pct_maths_1f, pct_maths_1s,
    n_reading, pct_reading_1f, pct_reading_2f,
    schoolweging
  )
Tip

  • Some schools only have 10–15 students in group 8. How reliable is a percentage based on so few students, and how does the "<5" replacement affect small schools in particular? (How) will you show this in your visualization?

  • Almost every school gets (nearly) all students to the basic 1F level. Which levels are actually informative for telling schools apart?

  • You do not need to worry about generalizability to other municipalities. The visualization should be relevant for this one municipality.

RQ2. Does the choice of test provider matter, even for schools with a similar student population?

Expand Instructions

Level: Inference

Target audience: policymakers, or the general public (think of a newspaper graphic).

Instruction: Schools can choose which of six providers’ doorstroomtoets their students take. The Volkskrant reported that schools using some providers get structurally better results than comparable schools using another provider. In this RQ, you will test whether you can find the same in the DUO data.

The raw test scores (*_GEM in eindscores) are on six different scales and can’t be compared across providers. Use the reference levels instead: they are meant to mean the same thing regardless of which test a student took. Choose one or more of the following outcomes:

  1. pct_maths_1s: share of students reaching the maths target level
  2. pct_reading_2f: share of students reaching the reading target level

Compare these between providers, while accounting for schoolweging – schools do not choose their provider at random, so a raw comparison between providers is misleading. Support your visualization with a statistical model (e.g. a linear regression with provider and schoolweging as predictors).

Data: 
# Run the data preparation chunk above first.

# Reference levels per school, with the school's provider and schoolweging
rq2_data <- ref_levels |>
  inner_join(school_provider, by = c("INSTELLINGSCODE", "VESTIGINGSCODE")) |>
  left_join(school_weging, by = "INSTELLINGSCODE") |>
  # AMN and DOE are used by too few schools to compare reliably
  filter(provider %in% c("LIB", "IEP", "ROUTE8", "DIA")) |>
  select(
    INSTELLINGSCODE, VESTIGINGSCODE, INSTELLINGSNAAM_VESTIGING,
    GEMEENTENAAM, PROVINCIE, provider,
    n_maths, pct_maths_1s, n_reading, pct_reading_2f,
    schoolweging
  )
Tip

  • First look at the raw differences between providers, then at the differences for schools with a similar schoolweging. Does the picture change? This comparison could be the core of your story.

  • A statistically significant difference is not necessarily a large one. How big are the differences, in terms your audience understands (e.g. “x more students per class of 25”)?

  • This is school-level data: you know which provider a school used, not the scores of individual students. What can and can’t you conclude from that?

RQ3. Do schools with similar test results give similar secondary-school advice?

Expand Instructions

Level: Inference

Target audience: secondary schools, or policymakers.

Instruction: At the end of primary school, every student receives a school advice for a secondary-school track (PRO, VMBO, HAVO, VWO, see the codebook). The advice is based on the teacher’s judgement and on the doorstroomtoets. In this RQ, you will investigate how closely a school’s advice follows its students’ test results, and whether this differs between schools with a more or less disadvantaged student population.

Relate a measure of the school’s advice (e.g. pct_havo_plus) to a measure of its test results (pct_maths_1s and/or pct_reading_2f), and test whether schoolweging moderates this relationship (e.g. with an interaction in a linear regression). Do two schools with the same results give the same advice, regardless of who their students are?

Data: 
# Run the data preparation chunk above first.

# Test results and school advice per school, with schoolweging
rq3_data <- ref_levels |>
  inner_join(
    select(
      advice,
      INSTELLINGSCODE, VESTIGINGSCODE,
      VSO:ADVIES_NIET_MOGELIJK, n_advice, pct_havo_plus
    ),
    by = c("INSTELLINGSCODE", "VESTIGINGSCODE")
  ) |>
  left_join(school_weging, by = "INSTELLINGSCODE") |>
  select(
    INSTELLINGSCODE, VESTIGINGSCODE, INSTELLINGSNAAM_VESTIGING,
    GEMEENTENAAM, PROVINCIE,
    n_maths, pct_maths_1s, n_reading, pct_reading_2f,
    VSO:ADVIES_NIET_MOGELIJK, n_advice, pct_havo_plus,
    schoolweging
  )
Tip

  • pct_havo_plus is only one way to summarise the advice. You could also turn the tracks into a numeric scale (PRO = 1, …, VWO = 6) and compute an average advice per school. Argue your choice.

  • Suppose you find that schools with a higher schoolweging give lower advice than schools with the same test results. What are possible explanations? Is it necessarily unfair? Discuss this in your report.

RQ4. Can we predict how many students get a HAVO-or-higher advice from a school’s context?

Expand Instructions

Level: Prediction

Target audience: school boards or the Inspectorate, who could use such a model to spot schools whose advice is unexpectedly high or low.

Instruction: Fit a model that predicts a school’s share of students with a HAVO-or-higher advice (pct_havo_plus) from characteristics of the school and its student population. Deliberately leave out the test results themselves (the reference levels): we want to know how much the context alone predicts. You can choose between the following predictors:

  1. schoolweging (how disadvantaged the student population is)
  2. spreiding (how much the students within a school differ from each other in disadvantage)
  3. n_students_school (school size)
  4. provider (which doorstroomtoets the school used)
  5. PROVINCIE
  6. DENOMINATIE_VESTIGING (the school’s religious or educational denomination)

You can be selective or choose all variables. If you choose to omit certain variables, please explain why. Evaluate your model on data it hasn’t seen (e.g. a train/test split) and communicate how well it performs, which predictors matter, and which schools deviate most from what the model predicts.

Data: 
# Run the data preparation chunk above first.

# School advice per school, with school context and provider
rq4_data <- advice |>
  left_join(school_weging, by = "INSTELLINGSCODE") |>
  left_join(school_provider, by = c("INSTELLINGSCODE", "VESTIGINGSCODE")) |>
  # special primary schools (Sbo) have no schoolweging
  filter(SOORT_PO == "Bo") |>
  select(
    INSTELLINGSCODE, VESTIGINGSCODE, INSTELLINGSNAAM_VESTIGING,
    GEMEENTENAAM, PROVINCIE, DENOMINATIE_VESTIGING,
    n_advice, pct_havo_plus,
    schoolweging, spreiding, n_students_school, provider
  )
Tip

  • DENOMINATIE_VESTIGING has 19 categories and some providers are rare. Categories with only a handful of schools make your model unstable – consider combining them.

  • A school with 8 students in group 8 will have a much noisier pct_havo_plus than a school with 80. How do you deal with that in your model, or in your visualization?

  • A school that deviates from the model’s prediction is not necessarily doing something wrong. Keep this in mind when you present the “outliers”.

RQ5. Your own research question

Expand Instructions

Level: Your choice (analytics, inference or prediction)

Instruction: If none of the questions above appeal to you, you can formulate your own. The DUO data should be the core of your project, but you are welcome to combine it with other data. This option gives you more freedom, but it also asks more of you: you have to make the choices that RQ1–RQ4 make for you. Before you start working on the data, make sure you have specified the following:

  1. A very specific research question. “Is the doorstroomtoets fair?” is a topic, not a research question. A good question names the outcome, the comparison and the population, so that it’s clear which plot and which analysis would answer it. For example: “Do schools in Limburg that use Route 8 have a lower share of students at reading level 2F than Route 8 schools in the rest of the country, when comparing schools with a similar schoolweging?”

  2. An approach. Is your question one of analytics, inference or prediction (see Levels of visualization)? Explain why, and what statistical test or model (if any) you will use to support your visualization.

  3. A target audience. Who is your visualization for, and what should they be able to do or conclude after seeing it? This shapes your choices just as much as the data does.

State all three explicitly at the start of your report.

Adding other data. You can look for other data to combine with the DUO files, for example DUO data from other school years (to look at changes over time), neighbourhood statistics from CBS (Statistics Netherlands), or geographic data to make a map. The school codes (INSTELLINGSCODE, VESTIGINGSCODE), postcode (POSTCODE_VESTIGING) and municipality (GEMEENTENAAM, GEMEENTENUMMER) are the columns you can use to join other data to the DUO files.

We recommend using AI tools to help you with this: to sharpen your question, to find datasets that could answer it, and to figure out how to join them to the DUO data. But check what AI gives you. Make sure a dataset actually exists and comes from a trustworthy source, check how many schools actually match after joining (and which ones don’t), and describe in your report which data you used, where it comes from, and how you combined it.
